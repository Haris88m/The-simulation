# Scientific Method

## Purpose

The project combines a possibility engine, forecasting laboratory, collective-intelligence experiment, and live ARG. These purposes must be evaluated separately.

## Two tracks

### Possibility track

Accepts unrestricted assumptions and evaluates internal consequences, interactions, sensitivity, robustness, and novelty. A fantasy branch does not need to become empirically true to remain a valid world.

### Forecast track

Contains only questions with a predefined outcome, deadline, resolution source, resolution rule, probability, evidence cutoff, and model version. Forecasts are scored after resolution and cannot be silently rewritten.

## Core methods under evaluation

- discrete-event simulation;
- system dynamics and feedback loops;
- causal graphs and intervention logic;
- Monte Carlo ensembles;
- particle filtering and sequential evidence updates;
- fixed-point and integer simulation kernels;
- agent-based and hierarchical models;
- proper probabilistic scoring, including Brier scores;
- calibration, sensitivity, convergence, and backtesting.

## Scientific discipline

The platform will distinguish:

1. software correctness;
2. numerical qualification;
3. empirical calibration;
4. forecast performance;
5. counterfactual usefulness;
6. narrative fidelity.

Passing software tests does not prove that the model predicts Earth. Synthetic coefficients must remain labeled synthetic until replaced or justified by versioned evidence and published methodology.

## No hidden definition of “better”

A future should be represented as a multidimensional outcome vector—health, prosperity, freedom, equality, stability, trust, sustainability, security, knowledge, resilience, and other declared dimensions. Players and collaborative worlds may use different value weights. The creators must not hide one ideology inside a single secret score.

## Intellectual lineage

The research direction draws on system dynamics, stochastic simulation, causal inference, particle filtering, probabilistic forecasting, agent-based modeling, computational self-monitoring, and compact deterministic computing. Frequently cited foundations include Jay Forrester, Donella Meadows and the World3 team, Daniel Gillespie, Judea Pearl, Gordon–Salmond–Smith, Glenn Brier, Philip Tetlock, and later work on agent-based and generative social simulation.
